## 1
English has one word, rice; Indonesian has three. ⟦nasi⟧ is cooked rice, the rice on your plate. Before it is cooked it is beras, the dry grain sold by the kilo; in the field, and while the grain is still in its husk, it is padi, which gave English the word paddy. Asking for more beras at the table is asking for a handful of raw grains, a little like asking for more cow instead of more beef.

For many Indonesians a meal means rice. Bread, noodles or a snack are not really eating, and you will hear “belum makan kalau belum makan nasi”: you haven’t eaten until you’ve eaten rice. (In parts of eastern Indonesia, sago, maize or cassava have long been the staple instead.) “Sudah makan?”, “Have you eaten?”, is also an everyday way of asking how someone is. Nak, short for anak (child), is how an older person kindly addresses a younger one.

## 2
⟦mudik⟧ means going home to the town or village you come from, above all before Lebaran, Indonesia’s name for Eid al-Fitr, the holiday that ends the fasting month of Ramadan. Every year tens of millions of people travel at once: trains, ferries and flights sell out weeks ahead, and the roads out of Jakarta can jam for hours, which, as Rina says, is part of it too. The word comes from udik, upstream, and so the countryside; pulang kampung, “going back to the village”, means much the same.

Jakarta, a city of about ten million, many of whom came from other regions, empties for a week or two: many food stalls close, and many household helpers go home for the holiday. Think of Thanksgiving or Christmas travel, on a far larger scale. At home, families visit one another, ask forgiveness for the year’s wrongs (“mohon maaf lahir dan batin”) and eat ketupat, rice cooked in a little case of woven palm leaves.

## 3
⟦Nyepi⟧, the Day of Silence, is the Balinese Hindu new year, usually in March. For 24 hours from six in the morning the island stops: no fire or lights, no work, no travel, no entertainment. It applies to everyone on Bali, visitors included. The airport closes for the day, hotel guests stay inside the hotel, and the pecalang, traditional village guards, patrol the empty streets; only emergency services run. In several recent years even mobile internet has been switched off. The name comes from sepi, quiet.

The night before is the opposite. The young people of each neighbourhood carry ogoh-ogoh, huge effigies of demons, through the streets with drums, gongs and torches, and many are burned at the end. The noise drives evil spirits away; the silence that follows makes them think the island is empty.
