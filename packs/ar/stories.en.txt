## 1
In the Gulf, coffee is how a guest is welcomed, but not the dark coffee most of the world drinks: Arabic coffee, qahwa, is lightly roasted and brewed with cardamom, sometimes saffron, and poured from a long-spouted pot, the dallah (دلة), into a small cup with no handle, the ⟦فنجان⟧ (finjān), often with dates beside it. The host pours only a mouthful at a time, and fills the cup again each time you hand it back.

Saying shukran, "thank you", does not stop it: the host simply answers أهلًا وسهلًا, "you're welcome here". To say you have had enough, shake the empty cup gently from side to side as you hand it back, where elsewhere you might put a hand over your glass. Take and return the cup with your right hand. In 2015 UNESCO listed Arabic coffee, "a symbol of generosity", as intangible heritage of the United Arab Emirates, Saudi Arabia, Oman and Qatar.

## 2
In Ramadan, Muslims who fast neither eat nor drink from dawn to sunset. The fast is broken at sunset with the iftar (الإفطار), and a last meal, the suhur (السحور), is eaten in the dark before dawn. To wake people for it, the ⟦المسحراتي⟧ (al-musaḥḥarātī) walks the streets in the last hours of the night, beating a small drum and calling the people of each house by name. In Cairo the custom is centuries old, and alarm clocks have not ended it: in many neighbourhoods he still comes, and at the end of the month families often give him money, sweets or the biscuits baked for Eid.

His call is Egyptian Arabic, not the standard language: اصحى يا نايم وحّد الدايم, "wake up, sleeper, declare the Everlasting one", which Modern Standard Arabic would write اصحُ يا نائم، وحّد الدائم. يا أبو سمير and يا أم سمير, "father of Samir", "mother of Samir", are how many Arab parents are called, after their eldest son; the standard language says يا أبا سمير.

## 3
⟦جحا⟧ (Juha) is the wise fool of Arabic folk tales, cousin of the Turkish Nasreddin Hodja; in Sicily, once ruled by Arabs, he became Giufà. In his best-known tale he sells his house but keeps one nail in the wall as his own, then keeps coming to visit it: at lunchtime, at night, whenever he likes. In the version told here he ties a dead donkey to it, and the buyer gives up and leaves him the house.

So مسمار جحا, Juha's ⟦مسمار⟧ (mismār, nail), is never a real nail: it is the small thing someone keeps in your life, or in a deal, as a pretext to come back and meddle, a foot in the door. Here it is the landlord's wardrobe.

The city is عمّان (ʿAmmān), the capital of Jordan. Without its marks it is spelled like عُمان (ʿUmān), Oman, so the story writes the shadda.
