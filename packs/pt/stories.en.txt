## 1
In Brazil, a ⟦cafezinho⟧ is a small cup of strong black coffee, often sweetened before it reaches you. Offices, shops, waiting rooms and homes offer one to visitors and colleagues, usually free (in a café or bakery you pay), and it is offered several times a day. Accepting is the friendly answer; turning it down is not rude, but your host may well ask again.

The ending -inho makes a word small, but here it is mostly warmth: a cafezinho is less a drink than an invitation to stop for a minute, much as "fancy a cuppa?" is in Britain. Brazilians do it to time too: "um minutinho" is "just a moment".

## 2
⟦saudade⟧ is the feeling of missing someone or something you love that is far away or gone, sad and sweet at once, with a wish for it to come back. It is famous for having no one-word translation; longing, nostalgia and homesickness each catch part of it. In everyday speech, ter saudades is simply "to miss": "tenho saudades tuas" is "I miss you".

Fado, the song of Lisbon and Coimbra, is full of it: one voice, usually with a twelve-string Portuguese guitar and a classical guitar. In a fado house the lights go down, the waiters stop serving, and someone may call out "Silêncio, que se vai cantar o fado!" ("Silence, fado is about to be sung!"). Talking during the song is bad manners.

This story is in European Portuguese: tu with its own verb (estás, sabes), pronouns after the verb (convidou-me, "invited me"), "a cantar" for "singing", perceber for "understand", and toda a gente for "everybody".

## 3
In Brazil, New Year's Eve is the Réveillon, a word borrowed from French. On Copacabana beach in Rio, two million people or more gather for the fireworks, and most of them wear white, for peace and a fresh start. Many also pick the colour of their underwear for the year they want: yellow for money, red for passion.

The white is usually traced to Candomblé and Umbanda, Afro-Brazilian religions whose followers dress in white and go to the beach at New Year to honour ⟦Iemanjá⟧, queen of the sea. They light candles in the sand and give her white flowers, perfume and mirrors, sometimes in little boats; if the sea takes the gift, she has accepted it.

Many Brazilians of any faith jump seven waves at midnight, making a wish on each, and some eat lentils or keep pomegranate seeds in their wallet for a prosperous year. Black is not forbidden, but many think it brings bad luck, and on that beach it shows.
