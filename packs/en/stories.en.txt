## 1
In Britain, "sorry" is not only for when you have done something wrong. People say it to get past someone in a crowd, to ask someone to repeat what they said ("Sorry?"), and when someone else bumps into them. When two people bump into each other, very often both say ⟦sorry⟧, whoever's fault it was. When the anthropologist Kate Fox bumped into strangers on purpose, most of them said sorry to her.

Talk about the weather works the same way. "Cold today, isn't it?" is a friendly way to say hello, not a question about the temperature. The easy answer is to agree: "Yes, isn't it?" or "Freezing!"

## 2
In the US, "How are you?", "How's it going?" and "How are you doing?" are often just another way to say hello, especially from people you don't know well: a cashier, a neighbour, someone at work. Many don't wait for the answer. The usual reply is short and friendly: "⟦Fine⟧, thanks, how are you?", "Good, you?" or "Not bad." The long answer, with your problems in it, is for friends and family — and for the doctor, who really does want to know.

Many Americans say "I'm good", though some grammar books prefer "I'm well". The story also uses American words and spelling: elevator (British English: lift), apartment (flat), neighbor and favorite (neighbour and favourite).

## 3
In Australia and New Zealand, "bring a ⟦plate⟧" means bring a plate of food to share: a salad, a dessert, something from the oven. It does not mean an empty plate. Visitors get this wrong often enough that it has become a well-known story. If you are not sure, it is always fine to ask, "What should I bring?"

Christmas in Australia is in summer, so many people eat a long lunch of cold food on the 25th: prawns, ham, salads, mangoes and cherries, and a pavlova (a "pav"), a meringue dessert that Australians and New Zealanders both say they invented. After that, there may be cricket in the backyard and a swim. On Boxing Day, 26 December, many people watch the cricket on TV.

Australians often shorten words: "arvo" is afternoon, and "BYO" means "bring your own", usually your own drinks. An esky is a cool box (in the US, a cooler). The story uses Australian spelling, which is like British spelling: colour, not color.
