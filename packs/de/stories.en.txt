## 1
In Germany, Sunday is a day of rest, and the law protects it. Most shops are closed: in many places a bakery opens for a few hours in the morning, and petrol stations and shops in big railway stations stay open, but supermarkets do not. What surprises visitors more is the ⟦Ruhe⟧, the quiet: on Sundays and public holidays, noisy work (drilling, hammering, mowing the lawn, even dropping bottles into a bottle bank) is put off until Monday, and a neighbour may well come round to say so.

The afternoon has its own custom: Kaffee und Kuchen, coffee and cake with family or friends. In England and Wales, large shops may open for six hours on a Sunday; in Germany most do not open at all, so people do their shopping on Saturday.

## 2
Vienna's ⟦Kaffeehaus⟧ is an institution, on Austria's UNESCO list of intangible cultural heritage. One coffee buys you a table for as long as you like: you read the newspapers, which hang on wooden holders, write, or simply sit. The coffee comes on a small tray with a glass of water, and the waiter refills the water without being asked.

He will not bring the bill until you ask for it, because that would be telling you to leave. Catch his eye and say "Zahlen, bitte!", traditionally to "Herr Ober". A tip of about ten per cent is given by rounding up as you pay.

The story's German is Austrian where the Viennese would say it so: Grüß Gott for hello, Jänner for January, and a Melange, Vienna's coffee with hot milk and milk foam, close to a cappuccino.

## 3
German has two words for "you": du for family, friends, children and, more and more, colleagues; Sie for adults you do not know well, and in many workplaces, especially with older people. To call someone du is to ⟦duzen⟧ them; to call them Sie is to siezen them. Sie goes with Herr or Frau and a surname, du with a first name, so the switch is a little like going from "Mr Lorenz" to "Klaus", except that the verb changes too: "Wie heißen Sie?" but "Wie heißt du?".

The du is offered, not taken, and traditionally by the older or more senior person. It often comes with a drink, clinked glasses and "Ich bin der Klaus", "I'm Klaus". Many start-ups and some large companies use du for everyone from the first day; more traditional firms often keep Sie. Once two people are on du terms, going back to Sie is rare, and would sound like a falling-out.
