## 1
“⟦吃饭了吗⟧？” (chīfàn le ma, “have you eaten?”), or just 吃了吗, is an everyday greeting in China, especially between neighbours and among older people, and most of all around mealtimes. It is not an invitation. It means about as much as “How are you?”, and nobody expects to hear what you had. The usual answer is 吃了 (“I have”) or 还没呢 (“not yet”), perhaps with the same question back.

It is often said to come from harder times, when whether someone had eaten was worth asking. Many younger people in the cities simply say 你好, or nod.

## 2
At a wedding in mainland China, many guests bring no present but money, in a ⟦红包⟧ (hóngbāo), a red envelope: red is the colour of luck and celebration. White is the colour of mourning, the opposite of a Western wedding, and money for a funeral goes in a white envelope, so a white one at a wedding looks like a bad omen.

The sum matters too. 四 (sì, four) sounds like 死 (sǐ, to die), so amounts with a 4 are avoided, much as 13 is in the West; some buildings in China have no fourth floor. 八 (bā, eight) sounds like 发 (fā), as in 发财, “to get rich”, so 600, 800 or 888 are welcome, and even sums are preferred at a wedding because good things come in pairs. So when the teacher says the friend 要发了, the joke is that he is about to get rich.

## 3
Mid-Autumn Festival (中秋节), on the full moon of the eighth lunar month, in September or early October, is a family festival like Thanksgiving: the moon, mooncakes, and the legend of Chang’e (嫦娥), who swallowed the elixir of immortality and floated up to the moon, where a rabbit keeps her company. In Taiwan, since the 1980s, many families also ⟦烤肉⟧ (kǎoròu, barbecue) on the pavement outside their homes, with the neighbours, or in riverside parks. The custom is often credited to television adverts for soy and barbecue sauce, above all Wan Ja Shan’s slogan 一家烤肉万家香, “one family barbecues and ten thousand homes smell it” (万家香 is the brand). Newspapers of the time suggest the barbecues began a few years before the adverts, which then helped them spread.

Pomelos are part of the night too: 柚 (yòu) sounds like 佑 (yòu), “to protect”, and many children wear the peel as a hat. The story uses Taiwan’s words where they differ from the mainland’s: 机车 for a motor scooter (the mainland says 摩托车), and 念书, more usual there than on the mainland, for studying. Taiwan writes dialogue in 「」 and uses traditional characters, which the app shows if you choose Taiwan.
