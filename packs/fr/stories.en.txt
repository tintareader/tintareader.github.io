## 1
In France, friends, family and often colleagues greet each other with la ⟦bise⟧: cheek to cheek, with a kiss in the air, not on the skin. How many depends on where you are. Two is usual in Paris and in most of France, but three is common in parts of the south (around Montpellier, for example), four in a few areas of the west, and in some places just one. Which cheek goes first varies too. Even the French ask each other “Combien de bises ?” when they move.

The bise is not for everyone. Many men shake hands with other men, and a first meeting at work usually starts with a handshake. If in doubt, let the other person lead, and lean in slowly.

## 2
On 6 January, Epiphany, the French eat the galette des rois, the “kings’ cake”, for the three wise men who came to see the infant Jesus. As the day is not a holiday in France, many families have it on the first Sunday of January, and bakeries sell galettes all month. In the north it is puff pastry filled with almond cream; in the south, often a ring of brioche with candied fruit.

Hidden inside is the ⟦fève⟧. The word means “broad bean”, which is what it once was; today it is usually a tiny porcelain figure, and people collect them. The youngest child goes under the table and says who gets each slice, so no one can choose the one with the fève. Whoever finds it is king or queen for the day, wears the paper crown that comes with the galette, and, by tradition, buys the next one. Bite carefully.

The king cake of New Orleans, with its small plastic baby, comes from the same custom.

## 3
⟦ataya⟧ is the tea of Senegal, and of its neighbours: Chinese green tea boiled with sugar, often with fresh mint, on a small charcoal stove. It is poured from glass to glass, from high up, until each small glass has a thick foam. There are three rounds, each sweeter than the last, and a well-known saying, found in several versions: the first is bitter like death, the second sweet like life, the third sugary like love.

It can take an hour or two, and that is the point: ataya is time spent together. Neighbours stop by for a glass. Leaving after the first round is a little like leaving a dinner before the main course. Senegalese call this welcome teranga, a Wolof word for hospitality and a point of national pride: the national football team are the Lions of Teranga.

French is the official language of Senegal, used at school and at work, but most people speak Wolof at home and in the street. ataya and teranga are two Wolof words that Senegalese use in French too.
