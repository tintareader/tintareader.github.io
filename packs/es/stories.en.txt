## 1
In Spain, lunch is the main meal of the day, often at two or three, and dinner seldom starts before nine. The ⟦sobremesa⟧ is the time after a meal when nobody gets up: the plates are empty, and everyone stays at the table to talk, sometimes for hours. Leaving straight after eating can seem rude.

## 2
On 1 and 2 November, many families in Mexico remember their dead with an ⟦ofrenda⟧, an altar at home: photos, candles, orange cempasúchil (marigold) flowers, whose colour and scent are said to guide the souls home, and the food and drink each person loved. The Day of the Dead is a celebration, not a time of mourning, and it is not Halloween, though the two fall a day apart.

When the visit is over, the family shares the food. Some say it has lost its taste, because the dead have taken its essence.

## 3
In Argentina, Uruguay and Paraguay, ⟦mate⟧ is both a bitter drink made from yerba mate leaves and the gourd it is drunk from, through a metal straw, the bombilla. In a group, one person, the cebador, fills it and hands it to each person in turn, always in the same gourd. You drink it all, until the straw gurgles, and hand it back. Saying “gracias” means you have had enough, so you are left out of the next round. Don’t stir the straw, and don’t hold on to the mate while you talk.

The Argentines in the story use vos, not tú: decís, querés and tomá are their dices, quieres and toma.
