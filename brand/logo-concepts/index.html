Logo concepts for Tinta

Tinta is ink in Spanish, Portuguese and Indonesian. The app colours every word of a text by whether you know it, and every concept below keeps to that palette: ink for a known word, pink for an unknown one.

Each concept is proved on the light and the dark palette: the mark, the lockup with the wordmark set in the reading serif, the app icon at 64, 32 and 16 px, a bare 16 px favicon, and the mark in one colour.

Two variations each on the shortlisted Drop and Tinta, reader, then four new directions.

Drop, varied

1aDrop, two words

The drop from concept 1 holding the line from concept 4: pink “tinta”, and the known word cut out of the ink. One mark for both favourites.

Works because
One line instead of two, so it holds at 16 px where the first drop lost its lines.
Watch for
The cut-out word is only as visible as the contrast between the ink and the icon ground.

Tinta, reader, varied

4aThe card

“Tinta, reader” as the word card: the unknown word above, its meaning beneath, left-aligned. The lockup is the card the app opens on a tap.

Works because
The stacked lockup is the most distinctive wordmark of the set, and the mark has more mass than two blocks side by side.
Watch for
Two stacked bars alone can read as a list icon. The top one has to stay pink.

A recommendation

Drop, two words is the one mark that carries both favourites: the drop for “tinta”, the pink-and-known line for what the app does, and it survives 16 px. Pair it with the card lockup, pink “tinta” over “reader”, for the site and the link preview, and the mark alone for the icon.

Of the four new, Ring of words is the one worth a second look: the least like anything else on a home screen, and its story is the tagline. Blot is the drop's wilder sibling if the drop feels too tidy.

Round one

The first six. Concepts 1 and 4 were shortlisted.

1Drop

A drop of ink with a text inside it: two lines, one word unknown. The name, literally, holding what the app does.

Works because
Reads as “tinta” at once, and the organic outline stands out in a row of square app icons.
Watch for
The inner lines close up at 16 px and leave a drop with a pink dash. Fine for a favicon, but the story needs 32 px and up.

2T of words

The initial built from the reading view: a line of three words with the unknown one in pink, and the stem hanging from it like the card that opens when you tap.

The clearest at 16 px, a monogram and the mechanic in one shape, and the nearest relative of the current icon, so the least risky move.

3Dipped bookmark

A bookmark dipped in ink. Reading and tinta in one silhouette; the pink meniscus is the ink line.

Works because
A silhouette nobody else has, still two clear tones at 16 px, and no letter in it, so it works the same beside Arabic and Mandarin.
Watch for
Bookmark icons mean “save” in many apps. The pink dip is what makes this one Tinta’s, so it can never be dropped in a one-colour use.

4Tinta, reader

The wordmark is the logo. The name is treated as a text in the app: to an English speaker “tinta” is the unknown word and “reader” is known. The mark is that line reduced to two blocks.

Works because
It explains the product on sight, and a wordmark-first identity suits a website and a link preview.
Watch for
The two blocks are thin on their own; on a phone home screen they need the wordmark nearby. The point lands only for English speakers.

5Stroke

A lowercase t drawn in one pen stroke, ending where the ink pools into a pink blot.

Works because
Warm and handmade, the most “reading a book” of the six, and the stroke keeps its weight at small sizes.
Watch for
The blot can read as a full stop after the t. The stroke weight wants tuning per size.

6Nib

A nib pointing down, its breather hole in pink and the slit cut through to the tip.

Works because
The classic sign of ink: formal, credible, and the best of the six in one colour.

A nib says writing, and Tinta is for reading. It is also the most borrowed shape here; several writing apps use one.
