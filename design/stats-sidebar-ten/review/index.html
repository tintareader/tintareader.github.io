The Stats Panel, Ten Ways

The three-question panel says the right things. Here ten designers each show exactly the same facts, in their own way, built into the running app in its light and dark themes.

What stays the same

Every design shows these facts, read from the app for the same reader and text, and nothing else: no new statistics, none left out. The three questions keep their order; each designer words the headings and lays them out in their own way.

Can I read this?
About 1 word in 8 is new to you. Readable with lookups, for an A2 reader. 86% known: 33 lookups in 262 words (227 known, 2 learning, 33 new). Marks at 60% workable at A2 and 95% comfortable.
What will help most?
The new words that come back in this text: monte 4×, aire 2×, enfermar 2×, escopeta 2×, cazar 2×, bicho 2×, cuero 2×, each with its gloss. A button, “Add 7 to Learning”. Knowing them: 92% of this text.
Am I getting better?
This text 77% → 86% since 18 Aug. Words known 1,751, +118 this week, each with its daily history.
Footer
A link to all your statistics.

The frame

  • 399 × 817 px in the reader’s right-hand column at a 1440 × 900 window, with no scrolling.
  • New words in the app’s pink, so the panel agrees with the text; every other colour is the designer’s.
  • Light and dark, from the app’s own switch. Status never by colour alone; text at least 10.5 px.

Ten designers

In the order of the work each is known for, from an 1869 flow map to illustrated data journalism. Each is a reading of the designer’s published work applied to this panel, not something they made or endorsed.

Charles Joseph Minard

Figurative map of a text

Flow maps, above all the 1869 map of Napoleon's army shrinking on its march to Moscow and back.

Minard drew a quantity as a band whose width is the quantity, and let it split as parts left it. Here the words of the text, the lookups and the words you know are bands drawn to scale, with their numbers written along them. Your progress in this text runs in a strip beneath, like his temperatures.

Can I read this?
The text's words enter as one band and split into known, learning and new, each as wide as its count. A scale beneath marks workable and comfortable, and a drop line shows where your share known falls.
What will help most?
The lookups run down as a pink band, drawn four times larger. Each new word that comes back peels off as a thread as wide as its count, with its gloss, and what is left is written at the end. The button and the share after learning them sit below.
Am I getting better?
The words you know form a band that widens day by day, with the last week bracketed. This text's share known runs as a line beneath, tied to the band by drop lines, like Minard's temperatures.

Type Old Standard TT (Alexey Kryukov, from Google Fonts), a revival of late 19th-century book and map faces, in roman, italic and bold.

Notes for choosing

Works: Every band is to scale, so the panel is honest down to the pixel. The lookups band losing one thread per returning word, down to 17, shows why learning them lifts the text to 92% better than any other design here.

Watch: The densest and tallest of the ten (811 of 817 px), with three band scales to keep apart. The paper sheet and engraved serif sit apart from the app around it. With the word card open, only question I stays in view.

W.E.B. Du Bois

One reader and one text, in three plates

The hand-drawn charts of Black life in Georgia that he and his Atlanta University students made for the 1900 Paris Exposition.

Du Bois drew statistics as plates: a title that names the subject in full, one bold figure in flat colour, and a small legend of coloured circles. Here each question is one plate on a parchment ground, or on the dark app ground in the dark theme. The text's words coil into a spiral, the way his population counts did, and every number is lettered in capitals next to its shape.

Can I read this?
The 262 words run in reading order along a band that starts at the left and coils into a spiral. New words are pink flecks in it, about one in eight. A circle legend gives the counts, and a proportional bar shows 86% known against the marks for workable and comfortable.
What will help most?
Each word that comes back gets a short pink bar with one block for each time it appears, then its count and its gloss. A gold button adds the seven to Learning. Next to it: knowing them, 92% of this text.
Am I getting better?
Two flat stepped shapes share one time line, from 18 Aug to today: this text's share known, and words known counted from zero. The last week's gain is green, with a green strip under the week.

Type Mohave (Tokotype, from Google Fonts) for titles, the large figures and the button: narrow capitals with a lettered feel. Sofia Sans Condensed (Lettersoup, from Google Fonts) for legends, glosses and small labels.

Notes for choosing

Works: The spiral is the most memorable picture in the set: the whole text in one coil, with the new words as pink flecks you could count. Flat colour and condensed capitals read quickly in both themes.

Watch: Glosses in capitals read more slowly than lower case. The spiral and the bar under it say the same thing at two resolutions. Its gold for learning words is not the app’s amber.

Harry Beck

Line diagram

The 1933 London Underground diagram.

Beck drew the order of stations, not the distance between them, and kept every line flat, upright or at 45 degrees. Here the text and your progress are drawn the same way: evenly spaced stops, ticks for stations, linked rings for interchanges, and a key at the foot.

Can I read this?
Eight evenly spaced stops, the eighth a pink new word, give the 1 in 8. A single line in three colours carries the 227, 2 and 33 words, with the 60% and 95% marks as stations and a tick where you are, at 86%.
What will help most?
A pink line with one stop for each new word that comes back. Each stop has one ring for every time the word appears, joined like an interchange. The line ends at the button and the 92% it would bring.
Am I getting better?
Two lines run from 18 Aug to today, one stop per reading day. They only climb at 45 degrees: one step for each point of this text, and one for each 100 words known. Both end in rings joined as one interchange: today.

Type Cabin (Google Fonts, SIL Open Font License), a humanist sans drawn after Edward Johnston and Eric Gill, for all lettering.

Notes for choosing

Works: One ring per appearance shows monte’s four without a number, and the line runs on into the button, so the action reads as the end of the line. The key at the foot is a real tube-map key.

Watch: Words known climbs in 100-word steps, so small days disappear. The map idea is strongest in question 2 and weakest in question 1, where the status line is an ordinary bar.

Dieter Rams

Instrument

Braun products from 1955 to 1995, and ten principles of good design: less, but better.

Rams made radios and calculators whose faces show only what is needed: a scale, a readout, one coloured key. Here the panel is such a face. Grey, white and black do the work, pink marks new words, and green marks the one key to press.

Can I read this?
A grille with one hole per word, in reading order, with the new words lit pink. Below it, the verdict, a readout of the share known, and a tuning scale with a pointer between the two marks.
What will help most?
The returning words in a table, each with a small level meter and a count. Then the one green key, and what knowing the words would give.
Am I getting better?
Two recorder tapes side by side: this text since the first day, and words known, with the last seven days bracketed.

Type Geist, from Google Fonts: a Swiss grotesque close to the Akzidenz lettering on Braun products. One family, with the light weight for readouts.

Notes for choosing

Works: The closest to something Tinta could ship as it stands: three modules, a graduated scale with a pointer, one coloured key. The grille shows all 262 words in reading order, with new words larger, so status reads by size as well as colour.

Watch: Bordered modules add frames inside a sidebar that already has one. The green key is the only colour outside the status colours, and green has no other job in Tinta yet.

Massimo Vignelli

One grid, three bands

Modernist designer of the Unigrid system for the US National Park Service, the Knoll and Heller identities, and The Vignelli Canon.

Vignelli designed everything on one grid, with two typefaces, few sizes and one accent colour. Here a six-column grid runs through all three questions, each headed by a black band as on a Park Service brochure, with big Bodoni numerals for the data and a grotesque for the words. The accent is the app's new-word pink in place of his red, so it marks new words and nothing else.

Can I read this?
The verdict comes first, in bold. Two big numerals follow: about 1 in 8 words is new, and 86% are known. One bar shows known, learning and new against the two marks, and the counts sit under it on the same grid.
What will help most?
The seven words make a ruled table in three grid columns: word, times, meaning. A total line adds up the times: 16 of your 33 lookups. The button sits under the word column, and 92% sits under the meaning column at the size of the numerals above.
Am I getting better?
Two columns, one for each measure, with the same parts in the same order: label, numeral, line, note. They line up with the two numerals in the first question.

Type Libre Bodoni (Pablo Impallari) for numerals and Spanish words, in place of Bodoni. Inter Tight (Rasmus Andersson) for everything else, in place of Helvetica. Both from Google Fonts.

Notes for choosing

Works: The strongest hierarchy of the ten: the black bands make the three questions impossible to miss, and the big numerals answer them before you read a word. The word table, with its total line, is the clearest list of the returning words.

Watch: The bands are heavy beside a text you are reading, more so in dark, where they turn light grey. Three large numerals (86, 92, 86) compete for the eye. It uses 798 of 817 px.

Susan Kare

Three windows, one bit

The icons and bitmap fonts of the first Macintosh, 1984.

Kare drew with very few pixels on a square grid, so that a small picture could be read at a glance and had some humour in it. Here every mark sits on whole pixels, in black and white, and each question gets its own small Mac window with a striped title bar. Pink is the only colour that matters: it marks new words, as it does in the text.

Can I read this?
The 1 in 8 is said in large bitmap type and shown as a line of eight word tiles, the last one pink with a question mark. A page under a magnifying glass, which finds a pink word, stands beside the verdict and the counts. One bar holds the 227 known, 2 learning and 33 new words, with tab-stop marks at 60% and 95%, as on the MacWrite ruler.
What will help most?
Each word that comes back is listed in pink, with one pip for each time it appears and its gloss. The button is the Mac default button with its thick ring; beside it, 92% in large type.
Am I getting better?
Two stepped pixel lines over a dithered grey, from 18 Aug to today, with small square handles at each end as in MacDraw. A pointing hand shows the way to all your statistics.

Type Jersey 10 (Google Fonts, SIL Open Font License), a bitmap face on a whole-pixel grid, set at exactly 1 and 2 pixels per font pixel, standing in for Chicago. Icons are drawn here as pixel art.

Notes for choosing

Works: The friendliest, and one of the most legible: bitmap type at whole-pixel sizes stays crisp, and a tiny icon gives each question a face (glasses, a bulb, a sprout, and a magnifier finding a pink word). The Mac default button could only be a button.

Watch: Three windows with striped title bars are a lot of frame for a sidebar, and the 1984 look is a period costume next to the app’s own type. Pixel type stays sharp only at whole-number scales, so it softens on some screens and zoom levels.

Paula Scher

Type at its true size

Pentagram partner; the Public Theater posters and identity, loud wood-type grotesques at clashing sizes, and her painted typographic maps.

Scher makes the words the picture. Here size carries meaning: the answers are huge, each question shouts its key word, and monte, which comes back twice as often as the other new words, is set at twice their size. Black and white, with the app's new-word pink as the one hot colour.

Can I read this?
A pink 1 and a black 8, both huge, say how many words are new, with the share known beside them. The verdict runs the full width in wide capitals. A solid band shows known, learning and new words, with ticks at the workable and comfortable marks.
What will help most?
The seven new words are stacked in pink, each set at a size in proportion to how often it appears: 13 px per appearance, so monte is twice the size of the rest. Each has its count and gloss. The button and the share after learning them sit under monte.
Am I getting better?
The first and current share for this text are set at sizes in proportion to their values, next to the words-known total. Thick lines on one date axis show both daily histories, with the start date and this week's gain under them.

Type Archivo (Omnibus-Type, from Google Fonts), one variable family used at three widths: extra condensed black for numbers and the new words, extended black for headings and captions, normal width for small text.

Notes for choosing

Works: The most energetic, and its size rule is true: monte is set twice as large because it comes back twice as often. It makes the returning words feel like the point of the panel.

Watch: “1 word in 8” and “86%” sit side by side at the same size, so at first glance they can run together. Wide capitals in every heading are loud next to a quiet reading column.

Amanda Cox

You are here

Charts at The New York Times and The Upshot that explain themselves through direct annotation.

Each question is answered by a headline that states the finding, then one small chart that proves it. Short notes sit on the charts with thin leader lines, so there are no legends to decode. Grey is context, ink is the subject, and pink only ever means a new word.

Can I read this?
The headline gives the answer: about 1 word in 8 is new, and at A2 that is readable with lookups. Below it, the text is drawn as 262 dashes in reading order. Notes point at a new word and a learning word and give the counts. Then 86% sits on a scale, with notes at 60% and 95%.
What will help most?
The seven returning words, grouped by how often they come back (four times, twice each), with dots for each time. A short line says they are 16 of the 33 lookups. The button sits on the left of the same scale, which shows the step from 86% now to 92%.
Am I getting better?
Two small charts side by side, labelled at their ends instead of with axes: this text from 77% to 86% since 18 Aug, and words known up to 1,751. On the second, the last week is drawn darker and marked +118.

Type Libre Franklin (Google Fonts, a free Franklin Gothic) for labels and notes; Newsreader (Google Fonts) for headlines and the Spanish words.

Notes for choosing

Works: The clearest of the ten. Each question gets a sentence that answers it (“Seven new words keep coming back.”), and the notes on each chart explain it where you look. The 86% → 92% arrow on the same scale shows what the button buys.

Watch: Headlines that answer (“Yes, on both counts.”) need wording rules for every case, kept true as the numbers change. There is a lot to read for a panel that is meant to be glanced at.

Federica Fragapane

Seeds, petals and sprouts

Data drawn as petals, stems and seeds, with thin lines and small careful labels.

Each fact becomes a small plant whose size is the number. The text is a seed head of 262 seeds, the returning words are the petals of one flower, and progress is rows of sprouts. A key shows how to read a petal, and every number is written next to its shape.

Can I read this?
The 262 words are seeds on a half circle, sorted known, learning, new. The arc runs from 0% to 100% known; the 60% and 95% marks are nodes on it.
What will help most?
Each returning new word is a pink petal. Its length is how often it appears, with one seed for each time. The button adds all seven.
Am I getting better?
Each day read since 18 Aug is a sprout; its height from the ground is the value. This text grows toward the 95% line, and the last seven days of words known are drawn thicker.

Type Raleway (Google Fonts, served from fonts/) for labels and numbers; the app's reading font for the Spanish words.

Notes for choosing

Works: The most beautiful drawing in the set. The flower makes the seven returning words one object with petal length as count, and its “how to read” key teaches that in one look. The arc carries 86% and both marks in one form.

Watch: A scale on a curve is harder to judge than a straight one. The “This text” sprouts start at zero, so 77% → 86% is almost flat. Labels around a flower take longer to scan than a list.

Mona Chalabi

Circled in pencil

Data journalist and illustrator who draws statistics by hand and keeps the person behind the number in view.

Chalabi draws numbers instead of plotting them, but the drawing still has to be right: things are counted, lengths are measured. Here the panel reads like a learner's pencil notes in the margin of a paperback, with the new word circled the way you would circle it in a book. It is ink on the light page and chalk on the dark one.

Can I read this?
Eight scribbled words on a ruled line, one circled in pink: that is 1 in 8, or 33 lookups in 262 words. The verdict is a drawn label. A hand-drawn strip shows known (hatched), learning and new words at their true widths, with the 60% and 95% marks pencilled across it.
What will help most?
The seven new words that come back, each with one tally stroke per appearance (monte gets four) and its meaning. A brace notes that they are 16 of the 33 lookups. The button is drawn, with a pencil shadow, but it is a real button.
Am I getting better?
Two hand-drawn lines follow the real daily numbers, with notes at their ends: this text from 77% to 86% since 18 Aug, and words known climbing to 1,751. The last week of that line is drawn over again and bracketed as +118.

Type Caveat for headings and margin notes, Patrick Hand for words and numbers. Both from Google Fonts, inlined by the harness.

Notes for choosing

Works: The warmest: it reads like a reader’s own notes, and the circled eighth word is an instant picture of 1 in 8. Tally strokes are a natural way to say “comes back four times”.

Watch: Handwriting faces are harder to read at small sizes, and Patrick Hand’s crossed 7 makes “Add 7 to Learning” look odd. The pencil texture is filter work that would need tuning on every screen.

Reading across the ten

All ten carry every fact, fit the column without scrolling and work in both themes. They differ in what they make you see first, and in how much of the panel is picture and how much is reading.

Nearest to shipping: Rams, Vignelli and Cox would need the least change to sit in Tinta. Rams brings the instrument scale and a grille of words, Vignelli the clearest hierarchy, Cox the sentences that answer each question.

Ideas worth borrowing whichever is chosen: Minard’s lookups band losing a thread per word (why 92%); Beck’s ring per appearance; Scher’s size per count; Chalabi’s circled eighth word for 1 in 8; Cox’s headline that answers the question.

How this was made

The numbers are the app’s own, for the simulated reader of the independent round: an A2 Spanish learner seven weeks in, reading the opening of Horacio Quiroga’s “La tortuga gigante”. The 1-in-8 figure, the 92% after learning the seven words and the week’s gain are arithmetic on the app’s figures. The A2 level, the 60% and 95% marks, the button and the link are that round’s proposals, not features of the app.

The designs were made by ten separate agents, one per designer, from the same brief and the same data object, so no figure is typed in by hand. Each panel is HTML and SVG placed into the published app in place of the Stats tab’s content, then photographed with Playwright in headless Chromium at 1440 × 900, 2× density. Fonts come from Google Fonts and are inlined, because the app’s Content Security Policy allows no other font host.

The designers did not make these. Each panel applies what their published work does to a problem they never worked on.

The scripts, data and full-size screenshots are in design/stats-sidebar-ten/ on the branch claude/gifted-faraday-dhccef.
