The Stats tab, five ways

Five designers known for statistics, each applied to the right-hand column of the app as it is now, with Julie Zhuo’s principles as the brief. This is the second round. It counts the taps a text will take and compares them with the reader’s own past texts. The first round used research thresholds for reading without help; switch Round in the bar to see it.

Round one, kept for comparison. It judged each text against research thresholds for reading without help: 95% known as the minimum and 98% as comfortable. Round two replaces those thresholds, because Tinta glosses every word.

The panel today

Same reader, same text, same moment as every mockup below.

  • This text shows 17 numbers and Overall shows 25. None of them says how much work the text will be.
  • Both bars are almost all Known grey, so the part a reader acts on, the 8 new words, is a sliver at the right end.
  • The headline is a share of words known, which undercounts what a reader understands: words not yet marked, and cognates such as hotel and taxi, count as New.
  • Overall gives precision without context: 8.17% of the dictionary, 69.33% of the Top 1,500, dates as 2026-08-24.
  • Names set aside still count against the reader. Lupe appears four times; marking it Ignored leaves the headline at 91%.

The version tried on 13 October said the share known, how far it had moved since the text was opened, and how many new words were left. The last two carry into round two, counted in taps.

The brief: Julie Zhuo’s take

Julie Zhuo led product design at Facebook, co-founded the analytics company Sundial and wrote The Making of a Manager. She has not written about reading apps. These are her published principles on data and metrics, applied to this tab; every design below follows the resulting brief.

Building dashboards is easier than knowing what to put in a dashboard.X, July 2021, posted in quotation marks

Decide the questions first. A reader brings three to this tab: how much work is this text, how does it compare with what I usually manage, and what should I do next?

Logging is cheap… But attention isn’t cheap. Most of what we track never helps.X, October 2025

Compute everything, show a handful. The panel shows the taps, the taps left and the reader’s own limit. The rest goes to the stats page or nowhere.

any metric will be a proxyX thread on goals, 2021

Lead with the goal. The reader wants texts they can finish, so the panel leads with a comparison to their own reading, “Lighter than most texts you finish”, and the numbers support it.

Diagnose with data, treat with design.X, March 2021, crediting Chandra Narayanan

Pair each number with what to do. “Five taps ahead” comes with the five words and a button to the next one.

Push, not pullParaphrase of Opinionated Intelligence, April 2026: dashboards only help people who already know what to ask

Push one insight. The app can work out that setting aside two names and learning mate saves 13 taps, so it says so.

exemplary analysis takes the listener through a storyX, July 2025

Order it like a sentence. Verdict, then the cost, then the next step.

The Nag MetricThe Looking Glass: an element’s share of the screen times how hard it pulls at attention

Keep it calm. No streaks or badges. Before the reading log fills, one quiet line says when the comparison will start.

Taps, and a limit of your own

Round one used the coverage bands from reading research: 95% known as the minimum and 98% as comfortable. Those were measured for reading without help. Tinta glosses every word instantly, which changes the conditions.

Glosses raise comprehensionComputer glosses in the reader’s language had a large effect in one meta-analysis, paper glosses a small one (Taylor 2006). Studies that glossed half the words or more had the largest average effect (Taylor 2014).

Looking up raises effective coverageDictionary use helped about as much as an equal rise in words known, and readers who looked up enough to pass 96% gained most (Prichard & Matsumoto 2011).

Words are not the whole textGlosses helped readers understand single sentences at every difficulty. Whether they helped with the whole text depended on how hard it was (Hosseini Alast & Baleghizadeh).

No tested limits for thisNo study sets level-by-level limits for instant-gloss reading. Beginners gain most from glosses in their own language, and length adds load of its own. Tinta can measure the rest.

Kept on the device. Tinta’s privacy policy promises no analytics and no tracking, and reading without an account collects nothing. So the reading log stays on the reader’s device, and each reader is compared only with themselves. Measured taps also avoid the undercount in “% known”, since nobody taps a word they know.

Five designers, one brief

Each panel shows the same facts in that designer’s visual language, built from the app’s own colour tokens. The comparison with past texts uses the illustrative reading log above; everything about the text itself is read from the app.

Stephen Few · Perceptual Edge

The bullet graphAuthor of Information Dashboard Design and inventor of the bullet graph

A dashboard is “a visual display of the most important information needed to achieve one or more objectives; consolidated and arranged on a single screen so the information can be monitored at a glance.”Dashboard Confusion (2004), restated in Dashboard Confusion Revisited
  • Two bullet graphs. The first puts new words per 100 against grey ranges from your own texts: easy for you, usual, and where you stopped. The tick is the most you have finished.
  • The second does the same for taps in the whole text, which is where length shows.
  • The scale starts at zero now, so the measure is a bar again. A table names the new and learning words.
  • One bullet graph: grey ranges for Hard, Stretch, Comfortable and Easy, a target tick at 95, the opened value as a hollow mark.
  • The scale starts at 85, so the measure is a mark rather than a bar.
  • A two-row table names the new and learning words. One sparkline closes the panel.
Through Julie Zhuo’s lens

Keeps: the most exact comparison, and the only one that shows both limits, density and length, at once.

Costs: two graphs and a table, and the next step is left to the reader.

Keeps: the clearest verdict of the five. Costs: the table leaves the next step for the reader to work out.

Edward Tufte · Yale

Word-sized graphicsCoined “data-ink”, “chartjunk” and “sparkline”

A sparkline is a “small intense, simple, word-sized graphic with typographic resolution.” And the question behind every number: “Compared to what?”Sparkline theory and practice; Envisioning Information, p. 67
  • The hero is the count of taps, 8, with “one in every 27 words” beside it.
  • A sentence carries the comparison: down from 22, five still ahead, lighter than 12 of the 13 texts you have finished.
  • A rug of your last 16 texts on one axis: finished as dots, stopped as crosses, this text as a diamond. Then the word strip, showing where the taps fall.
  • The numbers sit in a sentence: up from 89%, 95% with the six new words, 98% with the five being learned.
  • A range frame from 85 to 100 and the text drawn as a sparkline, tall where a word is new.
Through Julie Zhuo’s lens

Keeps: the rug and the word strip, each one line high, are the two graphics the brief needs.

Costs: a paragraph plus three graphics, and no button for the next step.

Keeps: the sentence tells the story. Costs: too much to take in at a glance.

The recommendation

Julie Zhuo’s cutHer principles applied strictly to the same brief

One panel through its three real situations. Before a reader has a log, it leads with the cost. Once it knows them, it compares. On a hard text, it pushes the fix.

  • Verdict: Stretch, with 91% beside it and a four-step ladder.
  • Next step: “Learn six words to make it Comfortable (95%)”, with chips and Go to it.
Why this one

Every number comes with something to do, the comparison is with the reader’s own texts, and it fits under the open word card. Switch the word card in the bar to see it.

Also in it: a nudge for new words read past without a tap (mole, riéndose). Marking them keeps Known honest, which keeps the taps honest.

It was the only panel where every number came with something to do.

To build it

  1. Count taps. Each time a word card opens, count it against the text, both in total and by distinct word.
  2. Keep a reading log on the device, per text. When it opens: its length, New occurrences and distinct New words, and words known overall. While reading: taps, words marked, the furthest sentence reached. At the end: finished (reached the last sentence), stopped, or reopened later.
  3. Learn the two limits from recent texts, say the last thirty, so they move with the reader’s level: the densest text finished, and the most taps finished in one text. Start comparing after ten texts.
  4. Offer the fixes that save most taps. An unlisted word written with a capital is almost always a name, so offer Set names aside. Then suggest the New word that repeats most.
  5. Nudge on words read past without a tap, so Known stays close to what the reader really knows.
  6. Keep it private. The log never leaves the device. If it syncs for Pro, the privacy policy’s list of what Pro stores needs a line for it.
  7. Remove the This text / Overall switch. Overall becomes the stats page, reached from the door at the foot of the panel.
  1. Count Ignored words as readable in the headline, so names stop counting against the reader.
  2. Keep each text’s coverage at the moment it opens for “Up from X% when you opened it”.
  3. Name the bands: Hard, Stretch, Comfortable, Easy. Round two drops them.

How these were made

The app is the published build in this repository (app commit 65923bd0), served locally and driven in Chromium. The reader is a Spanish learner set up with the app’s own “Mark as known: Top 500” plus about half of its Top 1,500 band, with seven weeks of seeded history: 615 words known on 24 August, 1,325 on 13 October.

La ofrenda, Tinta’s own level-1,500 story, opened with 22 New occurrences in 217 words. Ten words were then marked while reading (five Known, four Learning, Lupe Ignored), which left 8. Un mate en la oficina, Tinta’s level-4,000 story, is shown just opened: 37 New occurrences in 233 words, including Martín four times, Sofía three and mate six.

Every figure about a text is read out of the running app by script: counts, repeats, positions, glosses and bands. The one exception is the sixteen-text reading log behind each comparison, which is illustrative, because Tinta does not log reading yet.

Each panel was injected into the Stats tab using the app’s colour tokens, so light and dark are the app’s own palettes. Screens are 1440 × 900 at 2×. On this Linux machine the interface font is Inter and the reading face falls back to a generic serif; on a Mac they render as SF and Palatino.
