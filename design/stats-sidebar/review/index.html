The panel today

Same reader, same text, same moment as every mockup below.

  • This text shows 17 numbers and Overall shows 25. None of them says whether 91% is good.
  • Both bars are almost all Known grey, so the part a reader can act on, 8 new and 6 learning, is a sliver at the right end.
  • Total and Unique sit side by side with nothing to say which one the headline uses. It is Total, the running words.
  • Overall gives precision without context: 8.17% of the dictionary, 69.33% of the Top 1,500, dates as 2026-08-24.
  • Names set aside still count against the reader. Lupe appears four times; marking it Ignored leaves the headline at 91%.

The version tried on 13 October said the share known, how far it had moved since the text was opened, and how many new words were left. That pointed the right way, and the brief below keeps all three.

The brief: Julie Zhuo’s take

Julie Zhuo led product design at Facebook, co-founded the analytics company Sundial and wrote The Making of a Manager. She has not written about reading apps. These are her published principles on data and metrics, applied to this tab; every design below follows the resulting brief.

Building dashboards is easier than knowing what to put in a dashboard.X, July 2021, posted in quotation marks

Decide the questions first. A reader brings three to this tab: can I read this, what is in my way, and is it getting easier?

Logging is cheap… But attention isn’t cheap. Most of what we track never helps.X, October 2025

Compute everything, show a handful. The panel shows four or five numbers. The other 37 go to the stats page or nowhere.

any metric will be a proxyX thread on goals, 2021

Lead with the goal. The reader wants to read comfortably, so the panel leads with a verdict, Stretch, and 91% supports it.

Diagnose with data, treat with design.X, March 2021, crediting Chandra Narayanan

Pair each number with what to do. “Six new words” comes with the six words and a button to the next one.

Push, not pullParaphrase of Opinionated Intelligence, April 2026: dashboards only help people who already know what to ask

Push one insight. The app can work out that learning six words takes this text from Stretch to Comfortable, so it says so.

exemplary analysis takes the listener through a storyX, July 2025

Order it like a sentence. Verdict, then the reason, then the next step.

The Nag MetricThe Looking Glass: an element’s share of the screen times how hard it pulls at attention

Keep it calm. No streaks, badges or running totals while you read. Overall progress gets one quiet door to the stats page.

What 91% means

Vocabulary coverage is one of the best-studied numbers in reading research. Four findings shape how the panel should show it.

Count running wordsCoverage counts every repeat: 98% means one unfamiliar word in fifty (Nation 2006). The headline already does this. Unique counts belong on the stats page.

95 is the floor, 98 is comfortable95% is the usual minimum and 98% the level for reading without help (Laufer 1989; Hu & Nation 2000; Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski 2010).

Names count as knownLaufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski count proper nouns as known. Tinta’s Ignored words should count the same way, which makes this text 93%.

No cliff, so show bandsComprehension rises steadily with coverage (Schmitt, Jiang & Grabe 2011, 661 readers), and replications soften the cut-offs further (Kremmel et al. 2023; Webb et al. 2025). Bands are guidance, not a pass mark.

Tinta’s level stories are written so that a reader at that level knows at least 90% of the running words, which is why 90 is where Stretch begins.

Sidebar or stats page

The sidebar is read in a glance, mid-sentence, often under an open word card with about 500 pixels to spare. Everything that does not change what the reader does next moves to the larger page.

Five designers, one brief

Each panel shows the same facts in that designer’s visual language, built from the app’s own colour tokens. Use the bar at the top to switch theme, open the word card or see the whole app.

Stephen Few · Perceptual Edge

The bullet graphAuthor of Information Dashboard Design and inventor of the bullet graph

A dashboard is “a visual display of the most important information needed to achieve one or more objectives; consolidated and arranged on a single screen so the information can be monitored at a glance.”Dashboard Confusion (2004), restated in Dashboard Confusion Revisited
  • One bullet graph: grey ranges for Hard, Stretch, Comfortable and Easy, a target tick at 95, the opened value as a hollow mark.
  • The scale starts at 85, so the measure is a mark rather than a bar; a bar from 85 would exaggerate the gaps.
  • A two-row table names the new and learning words. One sparkline closes the panel.
Through Julie Zhuo’s lens

Keeps: the clearest verdict of the five. Range, position and target are readable in one look.

Costs: the table leaves the next step for the reader to work out, and the vocabulary row answers a second question.

Edward Tufte · Yale

Word-sized graphicsCoined “data-ink”, “chartjunk” and “sparkline”

A sparkline is a “small intense, simple, word-sized graphic with typographic resolution.” And the question behind every number: “Compared to what?”Sparkline theory and practice; Envisioning Information, p. 67
  • The numbers sit in a sentence: up from 89%, 95% with the six new words, 98% with the five being learned.
  • A range frame runs only from 85 to 100, the stretch that matters.
  • The text drawn as a sparkline: one tick per running word, tall where a word is new, with the reader’s place marked.
  • Set in the app’s reading face, foreign words in italic, a red dot at the end of the vocabulary line.
Through Julie Zhuo’s lens

Keeps: the sentence tells the story from opened to now to what it would take. The word strip is the best “where” in the set at 34 pixels.

Costs: three graphics and a paragraph are too much to take in at a glance.

Nicholas Felton · Feltron Annual Reports

The reading reportTen annual reports of his own life in numbers, now in MoMA’s collection

“Reduce, reduce, reduce… In most cases, I can get away with eliminating a key or an entire axis altogether.”Interview with Dan Meyer, 2008
  • One giant thin numeral with its verdict in spaced capitals, and the whole text as one hairline bar.
  • Three tiles keyed by a coloured rule: +2 since opened, 6 new, 5 learning.
  • The new words set as a typographic list with their uses, and a report footer with seven weekly columns.
Through Julie Zhuo’s lens

Keeps: the most motivating panel, with a verdict you can read from across the room.

Costs: the footer’s 1,325 words and +113 this week pull attention away from the text, and the tiles report without directing.

Otto & Marie Neurath, Gerd Arntz · Vienna, from 1925

Count, don’t scaleIsotype, the picture language made to show statistics to everyone

“At the first look you see the most important points; at the second, the less important points; at the third, the details; at the fourth, nothing more – if you see more, the teaching-picture is bad.”Otto Neurath, via Isotype Revisited
  • One sign is one word in fifty. Three rows on one baseline, when opened, now and easy reading, so you count the pink signs: 5, then 2, then 1.
  • Signs are sorted within each row, so the colours sit together and can be counted.
  • The six words as chips; words known as signs of one hundred each. Set in DM Sans, already in Tinta’s assets.
Through Julie Zhuo’s lens

Keeps: the plainest explanation of what 91% means. “Two in every fifty are new” needs no legend.

Costs: three rows to say one number. It explains more than a reader mid-text needs, so it fits better in onboarding or on the stats page.

Stefanie Posavec · London

The text’s fingerprintWriting Without Words, (En)tangled Word Bank, Dear Data with Giorgia Lupi

Her sentence drawings work by one rule: “a line would turn right after each sentence (scaled by the number of words per sentence).”Writing Without Words, 2006
  • One line per sentence in reading order, each dash a word, paragraphs as gaps. New words are thick pink with their forms written beside them.
  • ▸ marks where you are. A legend explains the rule, as on every Dear Data postcard.
  • The door to overall progress is a rhythm of days: a mark for each day you read, sized by the words it added.
Through Julie Zhuo’s lens

Keeps: the only design that shows where the difficulty is, and it could double as a navigator: tap a line to go to it.

Costs: the tallest panel, and it needs its legend. It belongs on the stats page, or as the Stats tab’s expanded view.

The recommendation

Julie Zhuo’s cutHer principles applied strictly to the same brief

  • Verdict: Stretch, with 91% beside it and a four-step ladder of Hard, Stretch, Comfortable and Easy.
  • Reason: up from 89% when you opened it.
  • Next step: “Learn six words to make it Comfortable (95%)”, the words as chips that jump to them, and “Next: rieron, six sentences on” with a Go to it button.
  • One quiet line for the words being learned, and a door to the stats page with no number on it.
Why this one

It is the only panel where every number comes with something to do, and it fits under an open word card with room to spare (switch the word card above). It also follows on from the 13 October version.

Borrow if you want more: Few’s bullet graph in place of the ladder to show the exact position, and Tufte’s word strip under it to show where the new words are.

To build it

  1. Count Ignored words as readable in the headline, so names stop counting against the reader. La ofrenda moves from 91% to 93%.
  2. Keep each text’s coverage at the moment it opens. The 13 October build (app commit d927aec2) already computed it for “Up from X% when you opened it”.
  3. Find the next New word after the cursor for “Next: rieron · Go to it”. The reader already moves word by word and can skip to unknown words.
  4. Name the bands: Hard below 90, Stretch 90–95, Comfortable 95–98, Easy from 98. Each needs a string in all eight interface languages.
  5. Remove the This text / Overall switch. Overall becomes the stats page, reached from the door at the foot of the panel.

How these were made

The app is the published build in this repository (app commit 65923bd0), served locally and driven in Chromium. The reader is a Spanish learner set up with the app’s own “Mark as known: Top 500” plus about half of its Top 1,500 band, with seven weeks of seeded history: 615 words known on 24 August, 1,325 on 13 October.

The text is La ofrenda, Tinta’s own level-1,500 story. It opened at 89% known. Ten words were then marked while reading (five Known, four Learning, Lupe Ignored), which took it to 91%.

Every figure on every panel is read out of the running app by script: the counts, glosses and bands from the Stats and Words tabs, the word order from the reader, the history from saved state. The only figure entered by hand is the 89% at opening, copied from the app’s own headline before any marking.

Each panel was injected into the Stats tab using the app’s colour tokens, so light and dark are the app’s own palettes. Screens are 1440 × 900 at 2×. On this Linux machine the interface font is Inter and the reading face falls back to a generic serif; on a Mac they render as SF and Palatino.
