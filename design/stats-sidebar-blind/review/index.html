Tinta Stats Panel

Five redesigns of the Stats tab in the reader’s right-hand column, each in the manner of a designer known for statistics, built into the running app and shown in its light and dark themes. Julie Zhuo’s questions shape all five. The last section recommends the panel to build.

The short version

The sidebar should answer three questions a reader has in the middle of a text, in this order, in plain words:

Can I read this? a verdict for your level, and how often you will look up a word
What will help most? the new words that keep coming back in this text, with one button
Am I getting better? this text getting easier over time, then words known this week
  • Build the three-question panel in the recommendation. It leads with a natural frequency (“About 1 word in 8 is new to you”), judges readability by the reader’s level rather than the 95% rule for unaided reading, offers one action (“Add 7 to Learning”), and fits under the word card.
  • Move the rest to a stats page: vocabulary by status, coverage of the commonest 100 to 4,000 words, full history, the text’s frequency bands and the notes on method. Drop “% of dictionary entries known”; at 10.80% of 16,213 entries it measures nothing a reader can act on.
  • Set the readability bar from the owner’s experience, then check it with data. Research puts comfortable unaided reading at 95–98% of words known; it says little about reading with instant glosses. The verdict uses 60% as the workable floor from A2 up, and lets A1 readers take on any short text.
  • The figures in the mockups are the app’s own, computed for a simulated reader. The level, the thresholds, the button and the stats-page link are proposals. Which numbers are real.

What the Stats tab shows today

The tab has two scopes. This text shows the share of running words known (86% here), two stacked bars for running and unique words by status, and the text’s spread over six frequency bands with a note on the OpenSubtitles list behind them. Overall shows dictionary entries by status, known words in the commonest 100, 500, 1,500 and 4,000, and a progress chart drawn from a daily history the app already keeps.

What to keep and what to change

  • Keep the headline share. It is the right number. It has no reference, though: nothing says whether 86% is easy, fine or too hard for this reader.
  • Two bars, one story. The Total and Unique bars repeat the same four-status legend; “running” and “unique” words are a linguist’s distinction, not a reader’s.
  • Frequency bands describe the text, not the reader, and need a three-line footnote. They belong with the text’s details on a stats page.
  • “1,751 · 10.80%” of 16,213 dictionary entries counts inflected forms and rare words in the denominator, shows two decimals, and can only discourage.
  • The progress chart is a good start (the history is real), but it has no scale, and at the default 400 px panel the date range breaks across lines next to the total (visible in the Overall crops).
  • Nothing suggests what to do next. The tab diagnoses and stops.

What makes statistics work, and look good

Six findings shaped every design. Each comes with what it means for a 400-pixel column beside a text.

Position beats angle and area

People judge position on a common scale and length most accurately; angles, areas and colour saturation least. So: bars and dots on one scale, no gauges or donuts.
Cleveland & McGill 1984; replicated by Heer & Bostock 2010

Natural frequencies and icon arrays

“1 in 8” and a row of eight symbols help people with low numeracy understand a proportion. So: lead with “About 1 word in 8 is new to you”, keep the percentage for precision.
Galesic, Garcia-Retamero & Gigerenzer 2009

A dashboard is read at a glance

The most important information, on one screen, monitored at a glance. So: the first two questions must stay visible when the word card is open above them.
Few 2006

Looks are part of the function

Embellished charts were read no less accurately and recalled better two to three weeks later; how good an interface looked shaped how usable people found it. So: character is welcome when it carries data (symbols, a mark per word).
Bateman et al. 2010; Tractinsky, Katz & Ikar 2000

Effort rises near a goal

People speed up as a reward gets closer (goal gradient). So: show a near, meaningful step (“knowing these 7: 92% of this text”), not a total against 16,213 entries.
Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng 2006

Act now, reflect later

Personal data serves different stages: collection, integration, reflection, action. So: the sidebar serves action while reading; reflection gets its own page.
Li, Dey & Forlizzi 2010

How top statistics designers work

  1. Start from the question, not the data. Zhuo’s “people problem”; Few’s dashboards built around what must be monitored.
  2. Match resolution to the question. One number for “can I?”; every word for “where?” (Tufte’s data-dense small graphics, Lupi’s individual records).
  3. Put a comparison next to every number: a threshold, last week, the reader’s other texts (Few’s bullet graph, Tufte’s small multiples).
  4. Make quantities countable. Isotype shows more of something with more symbols of the same size, never a bigger symbol.
  5. Use colour for meaning only. Here the product already has a vocabulary: pink new, amber learning, plain known. Every design keeps it.
  6. Say what it means in words (Lupi’s annotations, Felton’s captions).
  7. Make it personal. These are the reader’s own words and days (Felton, Lupi).

Julie Zhuo’s take

Julie Zhuo spent nearly 14 years at Facebook, rising to vice president of product design, wrote The Making of a Manager (2019) and the newsletter The Looking Glass, and co-founded Sundial, an analytics company, with Chandra Narayanan, who led analytics at Facebook. These are the things she has actually said that bear on a stats panel, with what each changes. Anything I could not check against a primary source is marked.

  1. Diagnose with data, treat with design.

    She credits the line to Chandra Narayanan. In GV’s interview she explains that data helps a team understand the problems and what matters most, and then you get into a design mindset to solve them. GV interview; The Looking Glass.

    In the designs: every statistic in the panel diagnoses something the reader can act on now, and the panel offers the treatment: the new words that return in this text, with “Add 7 to Learning”.
  2. What people problem are we solving? How do we know it’s a real problem? How will we know if we’ve solved it?

    The three questions from her Stanford talk “How a Facebook Designer Thinks” (November 2016). A transcript summary also has her steering teams away from business phrasing such as optimising a click-through rate, because that is not a people problem. Stanford eCorner transcript not opened

    In the designs: all five panels are organised as three reader questions in everyday words: Can I read this? What will help most? Am I getting better? No “types”, “tokens” or “frequency bands” in the sidebar.
  3. How will we know if we’ve solved this problem? What would be different in the world?

    From the clip “Defining Success Upfront” (Stanford eCorner, 2016). Stanford’s description adds that this keeps teams from defining success along the way with vanity metrics; that wording is Stanford’s, not hers. eCorner clip

    In the designs: what is different for a reader is that real texts get easier, so the outcome comes first: “This text: 77% → 86% since 18 Aug”. The recommendation also defines the panel’s own success upfront (below).
  4. Metrics are a proxy for the change we want to see in the world.

    A chapter title in the Prime Venture Partners podcast with her (2021). In a 2024 thread on goals she writes: “Any metric will be a proxy; THIS is the real goal.” Prime VP podcast; Thread Reader archive thread not checked on X

    In the designs: “words known” is the proxy; reading real texts is the goal. The panels keep the word count to one line and lead with the text in front of the reader. “% of dictionary entries” is dropped.

  5. Metrics and experience are not opposites.

    My summary, not her words, of her essay “Metrics Versus Experience” (Medium, 2016), which argues that setting the two against each other is the wrong way to start, that measurement shows how people really use a product, and that a product people value should move its metrics. Medium read from excerpts

    In the designs: statistics are part of reading, not a report to visit later; they sit in the reader’s column and point back into the text (which paragraph, which words).

  6. Two smaller points. On Lenny’s Podcast (2022) she said she set her book-writing goals in words rather than minutes, because you can spend 30 minutes and not produce a sentence (episode; via a third-party transcript). At Sundial’s 2025 funding round she described the aim as letting anyone understand and act on data “without needing a data science degree” (press release).

    In the designs: count words and texts, never minutes; plain language throughout.

Not used: her paywalled Looking Glass essays “The Paradoxes of Data” (2024) and “Elephants, Blind Men, and that One 15-Minute Trick” (2022), which I could only see in part. I found nothing she has said about language learning or reading statistics; applying her principles to Tinta is my work, not hers.

What reading research says, and where it stops

Coverage thresholds are for reading without help. Hu and Nation (2000) gave learners a fiction text at 80, 90, 95 and 100% known words and estimated that about 98% is needed for adequate unassisted comprehension; 98% was an estimate, not a tested level, from 66 students. Laufer and Ravenhorst-Kalovski (2010) proposed 95% as a minimal and 98% as an optimal threshold. Schmitt, Jiang and Grabe (2011, 661 readers) found comprehension rising roughly in a straight line with coverage, with no sharp threshold. A replication of Hu and Nation by Kremmel and colleagues (2023, 104 adult learners) could not fully reproduce the original result and advised caution with fixed cut-offs.

Glosses help. Abraham’s meta-analysis of computer glosses (2008) reported a medium effect on reading comprehension and a large effect on incidental vocabulary learning (as quoted by later studies; I did not see the paper’s figures). Yanagisawa, Webb and Uchihara (2020) analysed 42 glossing studies on vocabulary learning from reading.

The gap. I found no study that tests comprehension of glossed texts at 50–90% known words directly. One study described by Kremmel and colleagues glossed its target words and found no significant link between comprehension and the rate of unknown words secondary report. That is the space Tinta works in, so the owner’s experience is the best guide available:

“In my own experience it also depends on level of vocab, so A1 level and below any amount of unknown is manageable for a small text, then as texts get larger 60% known or more for A2 level is readable. B1 50% is a bit of a slog.”
The product owner

The app today aims higher. Its story writer aims for 88–97% known when it keeps a story within the commonest 1,000 words, and 93–97% above that (“~95% known … about one word in 20 is new”): the unaided standard. The verdict below follows the owner instead, and the panel always shows the count of lookups so that text length is visible.

Proposed verdicts. 95 and 98 come from the research on unaided reading; 60 and the A1 short-text rule come from the owner’s experience. Both are settings to check against how often readers finish texts.

The stats page, in outline

  1. What you can read now. Your texts by share known, today and a month ago; how many sit in your workable range (Tufte’s small multiples).
  2. Your words. Known and learning over time; weekly gains against your average; days read (Few’s bars, Felton’s report).
  3. The commonest words. Known words in the top 100, 500, 1,500 and 4,000, as bullet graphs with 95% marked (Few).
  4. This text in detail. Frequency bands, the paragraph map, unique and running words, every new word (Lupi’s word-by-word field fits here).
  5. About these numbers. What “known” means, the verdict thresholds, the frequency list, your level.

Five sidebar designs

All five sit in the Stats tab of the running app, beside the same text and the same reader: “La tortuga gigante” by Horacio Quiroga, for a simulated A2 reader who knows 1,751 Spanish words. Each answers the same three questions. Select any image to enlarge it.

Edward Tufte

The panel as a paragraph

Numbers live inside sentences set in the app’s reading face, and every graphic is word-sized: a thin bar of known, learning and new words with only the thresholds that matter ticked (60 for A2, then 95 and 98); five small multiples, one per paragraph, with a tick per running word and tall pink ticks for new words; sparklines for this text over eight weeks and for words known; a dot strip placing this text among the reader’s seven.

Julie Zhuo’s three questions
Can I read this?The first sentence: 86% known, readable with lookups for an A2 reader; one word in 8 is new, 33 lookups in 262 words.
What will help most?A sentence naming the seven returning words and the payoff, 92%. No button.
Am I getting better?Outcome first: this text 77% → 86%; then words known, +118 this week.

Strengths. The most information per pixel without clutter; it looks like the reader it sits beside; the paragraph rows show at once that paragraphs 2 and 4 hold most of the new words.

Risks. It has to be read rather than glanced at; numbers in prose are hard to compare; the dot strip repeats what the text chooser already shows.

Stephen Few

A monitor read in two seconds

A bullet graph carries the main measure: a dark bar for the share known, against grey ranges for the reader’s level (under 60 hard, 60–95 readable with lookups, 95–98 comfortable, 98 and up easy) and a target mark at 95. Below it a compact table, a bar chart of the new words that return with “Add 7 to Learning”, and the week in three rows: words added against the reader’s weekly average, days read, and words known with a sparkline. Greys everywhere except the status colour.

Julie Zhuo’s three questions
Can I read this?The bullet graph and a verdict chip, both readable without the legend.
What will help most?The returning words as bars, the 92% payoff and a button.
Am I getting better?Proxy first: words added this week against the average (118 against 142), days read, the total.

Strengths. The quickest answer to every question; every number has a reference; nothing decorative; the cheapest to build.

Risks. It feels like a business dashboard; bullet graphs need one look at the legend; at this width the 95–98 and 98–100 ranges are slivers; a week below average reads as a reproach.

Nicholas Felton

A reading report for one text

A report masthead (date, title, author, length), one very large number, then the text as 262 dots in reading order coloured by status. Three stat cells (33 new-word uses, 24 different new words, a 1:8 rate), a ranked list of the seven returning words with a dot per use and their glosses, the projected 92% as a second big number, and the week as three numbers over a column chart of eight weeks of daily gains; the day-one Top 500 import is outlined and marked off scale.

Julie Zhuo’s three questions
Can I read this?The big 86% with the verdict beside it, and the dot matrix as a picture of the text.
What will help most?The ranked list and the projection. No button.
Am I getting better?Proxy first: +118 words this week, 5 of 7 days, 1,751 words; the column chart shows rhythm, not reading.

Strengths. The most desirable to look at; a clear hierarchy; the dot matrix doubles as a picture of the text; the report frame makes a session feel like an achievement.

Risks. The tallest panel, so with the word card open it scrolls; big numbers invite comparisons between texts of different lengths; the date line is decoration.

Giorgia Lupi

The story, word by word

Every running word is drawn as a dash as long as the word, in paragraphs and reading order: grey for known, underlined amber for learning (as in the text), pink for new. A ring marks new words that come back; a thread joins the four uses of monte, the word for the bush where the story goes. Italic notes say what the picture shows, such as the paragraph thickest with new words. Progress is 42 stems, one for each day the reader marked words, with day one and the week away annotated.

Julie Zhuo’s three questions
Can I read this?A sentence: “86 of every 100 words here are already yours. The rest are one tap away.”
What will help most?The thread and the rings, then a sentence naming the seven words and the 92%.
Am I getting better?Outcome first: this story was 77% yours on 18 August and is 86% now; then the stems.

Strengths. The most human; it shows what an average hides (where the difficulty sits, which words return) and makes the reader’s own history something to look at.

Risks. It needs its legend and is slower to read. Some notes need writing per text: the densest paragraph can be computed, but “the bush where the story goes” cannot, unless it comes from an AI summary when AI is switched on.

Gerd Arntz, Otto and Marie Neurath · Isotype

Count the words

A headline in words and symbols: “About 1 word in 8 is new to you” over eight word symbols, one pink. Then the text as rows of symbols, each standing for 10 running words (22.7 known, 0.2 learning, 3.3 new; fractions are cut, not rounded), the returning new words with one symbol per appearance, and the reader’s vocabulary every two weeks with one book per 100 known words (613 on 18 Aug to 1,751 today). Set in Jost, a Futura-like face, as Isotype charts were set in Futura.

Julie Zhuo’s three questions
Can I read this?The natural frequency with eight symbols, then 86% and the verdict.
What will help most?One symbol per appearance and the 92% line. No button.
Am I getting better?Proxy: words known, as rows of books.

Strengths. The easiest to understand without numbers; it follows the icon-array research; the counting is honest.

Recommendation: the three-question panel

Build one panel that borrows the strongest part of four designs: Isotype’s natural frequency for the headline, Few’s scale with the reader’s thresholds and his action button, Felton’s list with a dot per use, and Tufte’s sparklines for progress. It keeps the app’s own type and colours, so it reads as part of Tinta rather than a new style.

  • Can I read this? “About 1 word in 8 is new to you”, eight word symbols with one pink, a verdict for the reader’s level, “86% known · 33 lookups in 262 words”, and one bar with 60% and 95% marked.
  • What will help most? The new words that come back in this text, most frequent first, each with a dot per use and its gloss; “Add 7 to Learning”; “Knowing them: 92% of this text”.
  • Am I getting better? This text 77% → 86% since 18 Aug, then words known with +118 this week.

Why this one: it answers the questions in the order a reader has them; it uses a natural frequency first and the exact share second; it judges by the reader’s level, which is what makes Tinta different from a graded reader; it offers one action that follows from the diagnosis; it measures the real goal before the proxy; and the first two questions stay in view with the word card open, as below.

The same panel on three texts

The verdict, the headline and the help section change with the text. All three are the app’s own figures for the same reader: one of Tinta’s built-in stories, Quiroga, and the opening of Don Quijote, where 53 of the 55 new words appear only once.

What it takes to build

  1. Already in the app: the share known, word counts by status, how many times each word appears in the text (the Words tab’s “Times in this text”), the daily history of known and learning words.
  2. New and cheap: “1 in N” (running words ÷ new running words), the returning new words (new words used twice or more) and the share after learning them, the week’s gain from the history.
  3. New and small: this text over time. The app already stores a time for each word’s status (for sync), so it can count which of the text’s words were known on any date; that is an approximation, because only the latest change is kept. Saving the share each time a text is opened would also work.
  4. Needs decisions: a “My level” setting with an estimated default; the thresholds; the bulk “Add N to Learning” action (the app already imports statuses in bulk).
  5. Clean the list: names count as new words today (“martín” 4× in Un mate en la oficina, “lupe” 4× in La ofrenda), and “aire” picks up “Buenos Aires”. Leave out capitalised words that are not sentence-initial, or put Ignore beside them.
  6. Accessibility: status is never shown by colour alone (labels, rows, the learning underline in the text). My colour-vision check of the app’s status colours found the closest pair to be new and ignored in the dark theme for protan vision (ΔE 8.4 in OKLab), which is acceptable with labels.

How we will know it works

Zhuo’s third question, answered before building:

  • Readers finish more of the texts they open, measured by verdict band and level. If A2 readers finish 60–80% texts about as often as 80–95% ones, keep 60; if not, raise it.
  • More of the texts readers open fall in their workable range.
  • Words added with “Add N to Learning” become known within a few weeks.
  • Not success: time spent in the panel or panel views.

How this was made

The app. The current static build of the site (built from app commit 65923bd0), served locally and driven with Playwright in headless Chromium at 1440 × 900 pixels, 2× density. Nothing in the build was edited. Each mockup is HTML and SVG placed into the live page in place of the Stats tab’s content, coloured only with the app’s own CSS variables, so the app’s light/dark switch themes it. Screenshots come from that page.

The reader. Simulated, not a real user. Spanish (Latin America), English interface. On 18 Aug 2026 the reader used the app’s own “Mark as known → Top 500”. Over the next eight weeks there were 41 more sessions (42 active days out of 57, including five days away), each importing that day’s words through the app’s Import, as a word and status list sampled from the app’s Spanish dictionary with frequent words more likely. The browser clock was set to each day, so the app recorded its own daily history. End state: 1,751 known, 105 learning, 7 ignored.

The texts. The opening of Horacio Quiroga’s “La tortuga gigante” (Cuentos de la selva, 1918; 262 words) and the first two paragraphs of Cervantes’s Don Quijote (1605; 217 words), both public domain, pasted in as a reader would; I typed them from the originals as I know them, so small differences from a printed edition are possible. The other three are Tinta’s built-in Spanish stories.

Type. Tufte uses the app’s reading face, Few and the recommendation the app’s interface face, Felton DM Sans and Lupi’s notes Fraunces Italic (both from Tinta’s Pro page), and Isotype Jost, a Futura-like face from Google Fonts. The screenshots were made on Linux, so the interface face renders as Inter and the reading face Palatino falls back to Charter; on a Mac they would be SF and Palatino.

Research. Web searches only; most publisher pages could not be opened from this environment, so several claims rest on abstracts, excerpts or secondary summaries, and are marked where it matters. This round did not look at any earlier design work for this panel.
